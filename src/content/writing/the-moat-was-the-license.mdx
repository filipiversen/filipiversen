A lot of legacy software is protected by a moat that no longer exists.

Switching costs, integration sprawl, regulatory familiarity, and "we have always used this" inertia are real. But the technical moat — the idea that the system itself is hard to rebuild — has narrowed sharply. Modern frameworks, design systems, headless APIs, payment infrastructure, document tooling, and AI-assisted implementation have changed what a small team can ship.

I just spent a couple of weeks proving that on a real category.

## The Setup

The target was an insurance policy administration platform. The incumbent is a well-known legacy system in the space with a seven-figure annual license cost and the operational gravity that comes with it.

The goal of the proof of concept was not to ship a marketing demo. It was to reach honest feature parity with what the legacy product actually does for an agency, end to end:

- Public marketing and landing pages.
- Dynamic quote flows tailored to product and jurisdiction.
- Online payment collection.
- Account creation and customer sign up.
- Document generation for policies, declarations, and notices.
- Full policy management across the lifecycle.
- Multi-tenant configuration that supports one agency with a couple of products or hundreds of brokers each carrying different products under a single tenant.

Hitting all of that in a couple of weeks of build time would have been unimaginable not long ago. It was achievable now because most of the foundation already existed before the first product ticket was written.

## The Foundation Was Built Before The Build

The reason this was possible is that the slow, expensive parts were already done.

A modern tech stack was in place: TypeScript end to end, a current React framework, a managed database, a headless API surface, a typed client, a deployment pipeline, observability, and a clean repository shape with conventions an AI assistant could understand.

A design system was already implemented in code, not just designed in Figma. Tokens, primitives, form components, tables, navigation, layouts, marketing sections, dashboards, and modals were all production-ready. They had the small details right: tabular numerals on numbers, hairline borders that respect dark mode, accessible focus, sensible defaults, and responsive behavior that works without per-screen guesswork.

That meant the team did not have to design and build the product surface during the build. We could spend almost all of the time on insurance behavior: rating, eligibility, quote shaping, policy state, document templates, payment hooks, broker hierarchies, and tenant configuration.

When people ask why the project moved so fast, this is the honest answer. The first commit was not really day one. The first commit was day one of the part the team kept buying back from itself by investing in the platform.

## A Headless API Made Many Surfaces Cheap

Insurance is not one screen. It is many.

A prospective customer needs a landing page, a quote flow, and a payment step. A new policyholder needs an account, documents, and policy management. An agent or broker needs an internal console. An operator or admin needs configuration. Carriers, accounting, and integration partners need data feeds. Increasingly, AI clients want a stable interface too.

Building each of those as its own bespoke screen-driven app would have blown the timeline. Instead, the product is shaped around a headless API. The same backend contracts power:

- Public marketing and quote flows with anonymous roles.
- Authenticated policyholder portals.
- Broker and agent consoles with stricter permissions.
- Admin and tenant configuration surfaces.
- Document generation and payment integration.
- Future MCP-style tool access without a parallel product model.

Headless does not just mean "we have endpoints." It means the product behavior is addressable without a browser, and every interface — public, authenticated, internal, automated — points back at the same contracts.

That is what makes a small team look like a much bigger one.

## Multi-Tenant Was The Real Test

A single-product demo for a single agency is not parity with a legacy policy admin system. The legacy moat is partly about the messy reality of how this industry actually operates.

So the proof of concept had to handle the real shape:

- One agency selling a small number of products, with their own branding, forms, and workflows.
- A larger MGA-style tenant managing hundreds of brokers, each with different products, commissions, document templates, and quote flows.
- Different jurisdictions and product variations under the same tenant.
- Role-based access for tenant admins, broker admins, agents, and policyholders.
- Tenant-level configuration of branding, pricing, document content, and payment routing.

The interesting part is that almost none of that required new "platform" code. It required disciplined data modeling, a clean permission boundary, and product components that could be configured rather than rewritten per tenant. The design system and headless API already assumed multiple consumers; tenancy is just another axis of configuration.

That is a different posture than legacy systems take. Many of them grew tenant features as bolted-on per-customer branches. A modern build can treat tenancy as a first-class shape from the start, because the cost of doing so has dropped.

## The Seven-Figure License Was Buying Lock-In, Not Code

Once you watch a small team rebuild a category in a couple of weeks, you stop believing the price tag is about the software.

The seven-figure annual license is buying:

- Switching cost.
- Long-running integrations.
- Vendor relationships.
- Compliance familiarity.
- Risk aversion at the buyer.
- The absence of a credible alternative.

Those are real, but they are commercial moats, not technical ones. They erode when an alternative appears that is materially better, materially cheaper, and materially faster to adopt. A modern stack with a design system, a headless API, and a sensible tenancy model is exactly the kind of alternative that makes those moats start to leak.

It is also worth saying plainly: replacing the software is not the same as replacing the system. Migrations, data, regulators, partner integrations, and operational handoffs are still hard. But the part the incumbent priced as "irreplaceable engineering" is not irreplaceable anymore.

## Where This Pattern Generalizes

Insurance is not unique. Several industries share the same shape:

- Long-lived workflows wrapped in older interfaces.
- High annual license fees defended by switching costs.
- Heavy regulatory or document overhead.
- Multi-tenant complexity that grew organically.
- A clear customer experience problem the incumbent has not solved.

Financial services is full of these. Brokerages, lenders, advisory platforms, back-office accounting, claims, benefits administration, and several payments-adjacent categories often charge ten times what the underlying software costs to build today, and they routinely fail at the customer-facing experience.

That gap is where the opportunity is. Not "we are a cheaper version of the legacy product," but "we replace the legacy product and also fix the experience that has been broken for a decade." A modern stack lets a small team do both at the same time, because the foundational work — design system, headless API, deployment, observability, AI-assisted implementation — is no longer the schedule risk.

## The Learning

The interesting moats in software are shifting.

Code is no longer the moat. Design systems implemented in code, headless APIs, and modern tenancy are commodity foundations for any team willing to invest in them. That investment compresses what used to be year-long greenfield builds into weeks of focused product work.

The remaining moats are commercial: distribution, trust, integrations, compliance posture, and customer relationships. Those are still real, but they are the kind of moat a competitor can plan against.

For categories where customers are paying ten times what the software is worth, that is the opening. Not to clone the incumbent, but to ship a better product on a foundation the incumbent does not have, and to do it fast enough that the price-to-value gap becomes impossible to ignore.

That is what this proof of concept was really about. Two weeks to feature parity is the headline. The real story is that the parts of the build that used to be hard are now infrastructure.
